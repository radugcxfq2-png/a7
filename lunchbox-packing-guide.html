The Lunchbox Packing Guide

A lunchbox that comes home empty is not luck. It is five small portions, packed in the right order and kept at the right temperature.

The short version

  • Pack five parts: main, protein, vegetable, fruit and one extra.
  • Keep wet food in its own lidded pot, away from bread and crackers.
  • Anything perishable sits next to a frozen ice pack.
  • Pack at night; add the ice pack in the morning.

The five-part method

Think of the box as five compartments, even if yours has only one. Each compartment has a job, and a box with all five filled covers energy, staying power and something to look forward to.

  1. Main. The starchy base: a sandwich, wrap, pasta, rice or crackers. Wholegrain versions keep hunger away for longer.
  2. Protein. Chicken, egg, cheese, hummus, beans or, where allowed, nut butter. It can sit inside the main or beside it.
  3. Vegetable. Something raw and crunchy travels best: carrot, cucumber, pepper, sugar snap peas or cherry tomatoes.
  4. Fruit. Whole fruit needs no packing at all. Cut fruit needs lemon juice and a lid.
  5. Extra. Yogurt, popcorn, an oat bar or a few cubes of cheese. Small, and the reason the box gets opened with a smile.

These are starting points. A child who plays sport after school needs more; a desk worker with a large breakfast may need less. The Lunchbox Builder shows the same portions for the foods you actually pick.

Keeping cold food cold

Chicken, egg, cheese, yogurt, rice and pasta salads are all perishable. They should not spend more than about two hours at room temperature, and a school bag in a warm corridor is well above room temperature by eleven.

  • Use an insulated bag, not only a plastic box.
  • Put a frozen ice pack directly against the perishable food.
  • Freeze a yogurt tube or a small water bottle overnight; it chills the box and thaws in time for lunch.
  • Chill the packed box in the fridge overnight so it starts the day cold.
  • At the office, move the box to the fridge as soon as you arrive.

Keeping crisp food crisp

Moisture is what ruins a packed lunch. Butter or spread right to the edge of the bread forms a barrier against wet fillings. Lettuce goes between the filling and the bread for the same reason. Tomatoes, cucumber and dressed salads travel in their own pot, and dressing travels in a smaller pot still, to be added at the table.

Crackers and popcorn belong in a separate bag or a compartment with its own lid. A single slice of cucumber is enough to soften a whole stack of crackers by lunchtime.

School rules and allergies

Many schools ask families not to send nuts or nut butters. Check the policy at the start of each year, because it can change when a new pupil joins. Seed butters, hummus and cheese are the usual swaps. If your own child has an allergy, pack their food in clearly labelled containers and remind them not to share or swap.

The night-before routine

Pack everything except the ice pack the evening before, while dinner is being cleared and the chopping board is already out. Leftover pasta, rice or roast vegetables go straight from the pan into the box. In the morning there are two jobs left: add the ice pack and close the lid. Lunches packed in the morning rush are the ones that end up as a sandwich and a packet of crisps.
